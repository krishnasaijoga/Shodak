Why Does Marital Rape Remain Legal in India?

In February 2025, the Chhattisgarh High Court acquitted a man of all charges, previously convicted by a Sessions court for sexually assaulting his wife — an assault that that resulted in her death from severe injuries. Why? Because marital rape in India is not criminalized.

Shockingly, nearly 6% of married women in India face sexual violence in marriage, yet only small fraction seek help — often due to deeply ingrained patriarchal norms.

Marital Rape and Concerns

Marital Rape is an act of forced sex in a marriage without the partner’s consent. While laws in India protect unmarried women, married women have no legal recourse.

Despite global legal advancements and Supreme Court observations, India treats consent as assumed within a marriage, effectively denying a woman’s right to say no.

Section 375 of Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), dealing with rape has an exception, that sexual acts with one’s own wife, if she is above 15 years of age, are not considered rape. This exception remains, despite the recent amendments.

With no legal recognition, survivors are forced to seek remedies under the domestic violence or cruelty laws, that fail to address sexual violence.

It is a blatant violation of Constitutional Fundamental Rights:

Article 14(Right to Equality): Violates equal treatment under law, here different for unmarried and married women.
Article 15(Non-Discrimination): Discrimination based on status of marriage as a form of gender based discrimination.
Article 21(Right to Life and Dignity): Violates right to dignity and bodily autonomy.
Though Constitution of India explicitly mentions equality, no discrimination, and right to dignity, yet we see a legal backing to marital rape.

Why Not Criminalized?

Legal Loopholes
Section 375 exception in BNS, lack of sexual violence provisions in Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act (PWDVA), 2005, no clear mechanisms to register cases, and burden of proof falls on victims — to show evidence of physical injury and coercion, ignoring psychological coercion.
Societal Resistance
Patriarchal definition of a wife as property of her husband, expectation to submit to a man, assumption of consent in marriage, and religious opposition of legal reforms in family affairs.
Lack of Political Will
Policy makers argue that marital rape would destabilize marriage as an institution and its values. Indian Government has told Supreme Court that criminalizing can be “excessively harsh”, and a man cannot be punished for rape within a marriage.
These arguments ignore the lived reality of countless women trapped in abusive marriages with no legal shield.
Fear of Misuse of Law
Opponents argue misuse of marital rape law leading to false allegations. This denies justice to genuine victims.
Limited Awareness Among Women
Women believe “Suffering Silently” is a part of marriage. This lack of awareness prevents from seeking justice.
Ongoing Debate
Supreme Court is yet to hear cases on this, highlighting ongoing legal and societal debates surrounding the state’s role in protecting women’s rights versus preserving the institution of marriage.
Global Perspectives and Best Practices

Most countries have recognized marital rape as a criminal offence, aligning with their human rights principles and gender equality.

USA, UK, Canada, France, Germany, and South Africa have criminalized marital rape with severe punishments.

In the landmark UK case like R. v R., 1991, the court ruled that a woman has the right to refuse sex in marriage.

International treaties like CEDAW (Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women) call for removal of marital rape exception in national laws.

UN Women, the United Nations Human Rights Committee, European Court of Human Rights, and Amnesty International urge nations to consider marital rape a criminal offence.

Developing Nations like Brazil, South Africa, and the Philippines have set an example by criminalizing marital rape, showing that it is possible even in conservative societies.

If other nations can do it, what’s stopping the world’s largest democracy?

What Can Be Done?

Addressing marital rape in India cannot rely solely on legal reforms, rather a multi-dimensional approach involving legal reforms, judicial accountability, societal awareness, and institutional support. The goal must be to ensure gender equality, protect survivors, and align with global standards.

Legal Reforms
Removal of marital rape exception. Inclusion of sexual violence as a crime in PWDVA and ensuring gender-neutrality in rape laws — to protect all victims including LGBTQ+.
Judicial and Administrative Reforms
A landmark, unambiguous judgement from the Supreme Court is the need of the hour. It can give a legal grounding to the cause. Consistent judicial interpretation to remove ambiguity from lower courts. Police and judiciary sensitivity training is required to handle such cases with seriousness and sensitivity.
Societal Awareness
Public awareness on ills of marital rape can help reporting of cases once criminalized. Incorporating gender and consent education in schools can help shift societal attitudes from a young age.
Institutional Intervention
Religious leaders, NGOs, and local bodies can help in shifting patriarchal norms in society.
Media in Changing Public Perceptions
TV shows, films, and social media campaigns can help normalize the concept of marital rape as a punishable crime.
Collaboration of Government and Civil Societies
In designing policy, support systems providing emergency, rehabilitation and trauma support services to survivors, funding for help-lines and crisis centers.
Establishment of Marital Rape Crisis Centers Nationwide
Dedicated centers in hospitals, police stations, community centers, and courts to help survivors be guided through sensitive processes. Free legal aid, medical assistance, and counselling to survivors. Financial assistance to achieve independence from abusive marriage.
Conclusion

Marital Rape is a public injustice. It persists due to political apathy, judicial inconsistency, societal resistance, legal roadblocks, and widespread unawareness. When law ignores violence inside a marriage, it legitimizes it.

How long must survivors endure this crime due to legal and judicial inaction? Marital rape is rape. Marriage is not a license for rape. No exceptions. No compromise.

It is time we shed this dangerous myth of “implied consent” in a marriage and take decisive action to protect constitutional rights, protect survivors, and no woman is ever sexually abused under the guise of marriage.
