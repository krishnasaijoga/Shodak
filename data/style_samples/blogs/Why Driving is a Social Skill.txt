Why Driving is a Social Skill?

When driving on a busy road, if we hear an ambulance siren, we see vehicles giving way. This situational response is not just a technical skill but a social skill.

The ability to drive a car and the ability to drive in traffic are two different skills. Let us explore why. From now on whenever I mention Driving, it means driving in traffic.

What is a Social Skill?

A social skill is the ability to interact and communicate effectively with others. It helps you understand your own feelings, and those of others, allowing you to respond appropriately in social situations. These skills come into play in various aspects of life, such as work, relationships, family gatherings, places of worship and parenting, helping us form deeper, more meaningful connections. It is inseparable from human life.

It includes skills like active listening, effective conversation, empathy, body language, conflict resolution, teamwork, and assertiveness. It is obvious that these come under social skills, but why is driving included in this list?

Driving: a Social Skill

Communication: We regularly communicate with fellow drivers through blinker signals, horn, flashing headlights, hand signals, emergency lights, etc.
Empathy: Giving way to emergency vehicles, stopping for pedestrians, changing lanes to accommodate faster or slower vehicles behind us, etc.
Conflict Resolution: Situations that put us in conflict are common on the road. We argue, fight, negotiate, compromise, wait for law, etc. to respond to such situations.
Compassion: Helping out a stranger by offering a lift, towing a broken-down car, assisting with a tire change, or providing navigation support.
Teamwork: Smooth traffic flow depends on cooperation of all drivers, whereas a single reckless driver can cause a pile-up on a busy highway.
Patience: Driving through peak hours not only requires skill but patience to move just a few centimeters per minute.
Awareness of Others: Yielding to pedestrians, respecting the right of way, and assisting those involved in accidents demonstrate heightened driver awareness.
Safety and Responsibility: Driving safely is not just about our own well-being but also that of our fellow citizens. Responsible driving, following rules, showing respect to others can help prevent mishaps on the road and foster a safer driving environment.
This makes a strong case for driving as a social skill. However, other activities like pedestrian commuting may also have social elements.

Does Pedestrian Commute Count as a Social Skill?

Individual Activity: Most of the time, walking is a solo activity, except for busy walk streets like Shibuya Crossing of Tokyo where teamwork and communication are necessary.
Limited Interaction: While we remain aware of fellow commuters, walking doesn’t involve signaling others about the direction we are taking.
Limited Communication: Pedestrian communication is limited to signaling traffic for crossing and not involved in fellow pedestrian communication.
Since pedestrian commuting involves limited interaction and communication, it may not be fully classified as a social skill.

Conclusion

While not every aspect of driving qualifies as a social skill, many element — such as communication, teamwork, empathy, and awareness of others — demonstrate its social nature.

The ability to navigate through shared spaces responsibly, considering others’ safety, and adapting to unpredictable situations makes driving more than just a technical skill; it is a social one.
