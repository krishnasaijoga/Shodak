Greenwashing: The Sustainable Lies Corporations Sell

You did your part by buying an eco-friendly t-shirt from a reputed brand. What they didn’t tell you? It took over 2700 liters of clean water to make it.

Welcome to the deceptive world of greenwashing where corporations sell the idea of sustainability, but rarely walk the talk.

And it’s not limited to fashion. It is rampant across industries like banking, food, electronics, aviation, automobiles, furniture, electric vehicles and ironically, oil & natural gas.

Let us understand what Greenwashing is, how it is done to deceive consumers, and how to spot them.

Greenwashing

Greenwashing is a deceptive marketing strategy that misleads consumers into believing a company is more eco-friendly than it actually is. It often involves blatant lies, misinterpretation of facts and norms, and over-inflating claims. It is often used by corporations to improve public opinion on brands and capitalize on consumers’ environmental guilt.

Take H&M’s “Conscious Collection”, marketed as sustainable but later called out for false claims, or HSBC’s sustainability ads that concealed investments in fossil fuels.

Why it Works?

Consumer Goodwill and Lack of Awareness
Many customers take tags like “eco-friendly”, “sustainable”, “organic”, or “recyclable” at face value when mentioned by big corporates. This is misused by corporations to capture consumers’ trust and lack of expertise.
Information Asymmetry
Corporations control the narrative by glorifying minimal acts of sustainability like planting trees and hiding acts like lobbying against climate change behind closed doors.
Lack of Regulation and Oversight
Environmental laws vary in different countries, so corporations use this ambiguity to operate in a gray area and misinterpret claims.
Take the example of Anglian Water in the UK, which promoted recycling using wildlife-friendly wetlands while concealing the discharge of 824 sewage spills into rivers daily.
Media and Marketing Power
Corporations have vast marketing budgets. A flashy sustainability campaign gets more visibility than actual scientific reports. Greenwashing thrives in a world of perception.
Investor Appeal
With the rise in green funding, even a green label, even superficial, can improve access to funds.
Why Greenwashing is Dangerous?

Delays Action
It creates an illusion of progress, reducing pressure for real change.
Undermines Trust
Once these campaigns are called out by climate organizations and the public, these corporations lose public trust. This can fuel public apathy or even climate denial.
Diverts Resources
Governments can divert subsidies to corporations that don’t deliver, leaving no space for real change makers.
Policy Distortion
Greenwashing corporations often lobby for weaker climate change regulations. A study revealed that Glencore, ExxonMobil, and Stellantis are lobbying for weaker climate change policies though pledged publicly for reducing emissions.
Carbon Accounting Fictions
Carbon emission underreporting and inflating carbon offsets can hinder tracking of climate change impact real time.
Spotting Greenwashing

Unverified or Vague Buzzwords
Buzzwords like “green”, “sustainable”, “eco-friendly”, or “Net-Zero” do not make sense without a standard definition or context. Question the corporations for definitions, metrics, and third-party validations.
Lack of Proof or Vague Disclosures
No documentation or certifications from reputed institutes is a red flag.
Tiny Green Efforts, Big PR
Check if core operations of the corporation have changed and not efforts like planting trees for $1 schemes.
Pledge Without a Plan
Companies like Indian Oil pledged net zero by 2046 but offered a vague roadmap for reducing emissions compared to Google.
Misleading Visuals
A lot of green visuals like trees, animals, or nature imagery doesn’t equal emission reduction. Check the company’s sustainability report, audit reports, impact disclosures, or lifecycle assessments.
Hiding Key Facts
No public information on workplace conditions, water usage, packaging information, or carbon footprint means shady business.
Carbon Offset Gimmicks
Companies claim Zero-Emissions by buying offsets and not changing core business operations to reduce emissions. Look for reduction impact at source and verified offsets that are not double counted.
Inconsistency Across Products
Companies can have one green brand while the rest of their portfolio remains unsustainable. Example of Coca-Cola heavily promotes “World Without Waste” campaign but still relies on sales of products that produce plastic waste.
Checking Reputable Resources
Use resources like Environmental Working Group, Climate Action 100+, Carbon Disclosure Project (CDP), Greenwashing Index, Changing Markets Foundation, and Greenpeace NGO.
Tackling Greenwashing

Regulate and Enforce Strictly
Clear & unambiguous definitions, mandatory third-party carbon audit, and scientific link to claims mandate in advertising.
Empower Consumers
Educate consumers, provide clear resources, and create standardized certifications to help them spot greenwashing. Articles like this aim to do that.
Strengthen Independent Watchdogs
Groups like Greenpeace, Earth.org, and InfluenceMap uncover greenwashing claims. So encourage their findings, work, and courage.
Change Business Culture
Businesses must treat sustainability as a core principle, not just a marketing tool. They can follow verified frameworks from Global Reporting Initiative (GRI), CDP, and Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosure (TCFD). And can also collaborate with UN to drive innovation in sustainable business practices.
Greenwashing thrives on growing consumer demand for sustainable products, leveraging false narratives in marketing. However, this threatens corporation credibility, exploits consumers, undermines genuine climate efforts, and accelerates environmental degradation.

The solution? Education, regulation, watchdog activism, and real transparency.

Greenwashing isn’t just a corporate PR issue — it’s a barrier to climate solutions. As consumers, we hold the right to demand truth. Let’s use it.
